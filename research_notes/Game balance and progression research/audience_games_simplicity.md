## Q1. What does the research on audience-participation games (APGs) say about roles, feedback, rewards and latency?

### Takeaway
The academic APG literature (Seering, Hammer, Glickman, Striner, Lessel, mostly CMU and Saarland) consistently names **latency, a shared screen, attention management, agency and the audience-streamer relationship** as the core design problems. It treats audiences as mixed populations: some help, some seek power, some troll. Social motives (community, interaction, being noticed) predict Twitch engagement better than any other motive studied. Crowd-control studies (Twitch Plays Pokémon) show that aggregated, timed votes are how a large crowd acts coherently, and that vote rules polarise communities.

### Cited Findings
**Foundational APG papers (research)**
- The paper the brief calls "Shifting Design Perspectives on Audience Participation Games" (Seering et al. 2017) could not be found under that title. The closest match is **Seering, Savage, Eagle, Churchin, Moeller, Bigham & Hammer, "Audience Participation Games: Blurring the Line Between Player and Spectator," DIS 2017.** It is probably the intended paper, but the citation should be checked. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317417086_Audience_Participation_Games_Blurring_the_Line_Between_Player_and_Spectator); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Audience-Participation-Games:-Blurring-the-Line-and-Seering-Savage/feb38af3180f56d8d894b3a0e1af8c8335d0afda)
- Seering et al. built several versions of two prototype games as design probes and livestreamed them. From that they developed a framework of audience motivations and participation styles, and they studied how mechanics shape an audience member's sense of agency. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Audience-Participation-Games:-Blurring-the-Line-and-Seering-Savage/feb38af3180f56d8d894b3a0e1af8c8335d0afda)
- The paper distinguishes the **player** (the primary agent, with a consistent, direct role) from the **audience member** (contributes but is not essential to progression). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Audience-Participation-Games:-Blurring-the-Line-and-Seering-Savage/feb38af3180f56d8d894b3a0e1af8c8335d0afda)
- A later paper that cites this work groups audience participants into **five types: helpers, power seekers, collaborators, solipsists and trolls.** This came through a secondary citation; the exact attribution is unverified. — [Game On, Chat! (DiVA thesis)](http://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1977294/FULLTEXT02.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Audience-Participation-Games:-Blurring-the-Line-and-Seering-Savage/feb38af3180f56d8d894b3a0e1af8c8335d0afda)
- **Glickman, McKenzie, Seering, Moeller & Hammer, "Design Challenges for Livestreamed Audience Participation Games," CHI PLAY 2018.** Three game teams got an audience-participation toolkit and built APGs over ten months. The paper names **six challenges: latency, screen sharing, attention management, player agency, audience-streamer relationships, and shifting schedules.** — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3242671.3242708); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Design-Challenges-for-Livestreamed-Audience-Games-Glickman-McKenzie/0936c753cb30ebebcd3c5bba6193e95fb642cdf2)
- **Striner, Webb, Hammer & Cook, "Mapping Design Spaces for Audience Participation in Game Live Streaming," CHI 2021.** The authors thematically analysed student APG design documents and organised the space into three dimensions, **Agency, Pacing and Community.** A citing summary notes that viewers compete for limited streamer attention, which works as a form of validation. — [ACM DL full HTML](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445511); [Memphis Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/2954/)
- **Lessel, Mauderer, Wolff & Krüger, "Let's Play My Way," TVX 2017.** In a TPP-like test, viewers shared control of one character through aggregated chat. The authors found that offering a wider range of influence techniques than the original TPP helps the audience organise itself in more nuanced ways. — [DFKI record](https://dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/9088); [preprint](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/paper_lets_play_my_way_investigating_audience_influence_in_user_generated_gaming_livestreams.pdf)
- **Lessel et al., "Streamer's Hell," CHI PLAY 2022.** In an online study (n=81), viewers were split on letting chat influence things beyond the game, such as room lighting or vibration on the streamer's arm. Options that interfered with the streamer were less favoured, and perception depended on the game and on why the streamer was playing. In a lab study, six streamers saw the appeal but wanted customisation and control. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3549515); [preprint](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/Lessel-StreamersHell.pdf)

**Viewer motivation (research)**
- **Hilvert-Bruce et al. 2018** (Computers in Human Behavior 84:58-67): social motivations were the strongest predictors of Twitch engagement. **Sense of community and social interaction were the only motivators that predicted all engagement types studied** (emotional connectedness, watch time, subscription length, donations). This came through secondary summaries. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218300712); [Tampere Gamification Group summary](https://webpages.tuni.fi/gamification/2018/09/14/social-motivations-of-live-streaming-viewer-engagement-on-twitch/)
- **Hamilton et al. 2014** is cited as finding that two key engagement factors are perceived streamer friendliness and a unique social atmosphere specific to the stream. This came through secondary citation. — [ResearchGate (Hilvert-Bruce)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323192870_Social_motivations_of_live-streaming_viewer_engagement_on_Twitch)
- A paper citing this line of work claims that streamers who acknowledged viewers **by name** produced significantly stronger parasocial relationships. This is a **secondhand claim, not verified** against its original source. — [ResearchGate (Hilvert-Bruce listing)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323192870_Social_motivations_of_live-streaming_viewer_engagement_on_Twitch)

**Crowd control and Twitch Plays Pokémon (research and reportage)**
- **Aleta & Moreno 2019** (EPJ Data Science) analysed the original TPP. Players who do **not** follow the crowd's average behaviour were key to progress, and the anarchy/democracy mode vote **clearly polarised the community, splitting it in two.** — [EPJ Data Science](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-019-0200-1); [BMC On Society blog](https://blogs.biomedcentral.com/on-society/2019/06/25/what-twitch-plays-pokemon-tells-us-about-crowd-behavior/)
- In TPP, **Anarchy** executed every input in sequence. **Democracy** was added after the crowd got stuck on a puzzle; it tallied inputs over a window and ran the most-voted command. Sources give the window as 20s or 5s, probably from different phases. Switching to Democracy needed a supermajority (75-80% depending on season), and switching back needed only a majority. About 658,000 participants finished Pokémon Red in 16 days. — [Wikipedia: Twitch Plays Pokémon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitch_Plays_Pok%C3%A9mon); [Bulbapedia Season 1](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Twitch_Plays_Pok%c3%a9mon/Season_1); [Medium explainer (opinion-level source)](https://nickyliu91.medium.com/twitch-plays-pokemon-db0c1980ccc3)
- TPP's input lag was reported at roughly **20-40 seconds** between a viewer typing "left" and the character moving. This is reportage, and the exact source within the result set is unconfirmed. — [PLU Mast Media](https://mastmedia.plu.edu/2014/twitch-plays-pokemon-thousands-of-users-simultaneously-play-pokemon-red-in-video-game-social-experiment/); [MIT Game Lab](http://gamelab.mit.edu/deities-democracy-and-disaster-twitch-plays-pokemon/)

**Latency in general**
- A Twitch developer-forum thread uses **Choice Chamber** as the reference example of interactivity designed around broadcast delay: viewers vote in chat and the tallies shape the run. — [Twitch dev forum](https://discuss.dev.twitch.com/t/delay-adjusted-game-example/23954)
- A CMU ETC student project postmortem says that balancing direct control against inherent broadcast delay is hard. A Utrecht thesis lists stream delay, an arbitrary number of viewers and UI design as the core challenges. (Student work.) — [CMU ETC postmortem](https://www.etc.cmu.edu/projects/commit-to-the-bits/index.php/2019/04/29/week-15-post-mortem); [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/43671)
- Streaming vendors describe typical RTMP-to-viewer delay as about 10 seconds, and up to about 20. These are vendor support pages, not measurements. — [MyOwnConference](https://myownconference.com/support/en/rooms/broadcast-latency); [Superpeer help](https://help.superpeer.com/en/articles/5215129-is-there-a-delay-between-me-and-my-audience)

### Inferences
- Corrupted Raids already follows the research consensus where it matters most. Viewers are audience-tier participants: their character is in the fight, but they do not steer it in real time. The fight auto-resolves, so latency cannot break it. Door votes are aggregated over a timed window, which is the Democracy-mode pattern. The 20s `CHOICE_WINDOW_MS` sits at the upper end of the reported delay range, which is appropriate.
- The "helpers / power seekers / trolls" taxonomy argues for giving each motive a legitimate outlet. Helpers get to tank or heal for the party. Power seekers get gear and Corruption. Recognition-seekers get named on screen, for example through a "most recently hit" roster and a daily title. Trolls get nothing that can wreck other people's nights: the door vote is party-only, which matches the AGENTS.md rule that only the party votes.
- Hilvert-Bruce's result (community and interaction predict all engagement) suggests the social layer is not decoration. Showing **names** on stream ("X took the hit," "Dungeoneer of the day: Y") may be among the highest-value, lowest-complexity features available. The by-name acknowledgement claim supports this but is unverified.
- TPP's polarisation finding is a warning against vote mechanics that split chat into permanent factions. Door votes reset every round, which avoids that.

### Gaps
- The full texts of Seering 2017, Glickman 2018 and Striner 2021 were not readable. Their specific design recommendations (for example, how they mitigated latency, or what made an audience feel agency) are not captured here.
- **No source was found for Glickman et al. on "Twitch audience roles"** as a separate paper. The Glickman work found is the 2018 design-challenges paper.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that measures late-joiner effects or join-window length in APGs.

## Q2. How do shipped audience games handle join windows, late joiners, rewards for participating vs winning, and leaderboards?

### Takeaway
Shipped Twitch games converge on a few patterns. There is a **clearly opened and closed join window**, triggered by a chat command and prompted on screen. **Viewers have no control once the action starts**: Marbles, Stream Raiders and Corrupted Raids are all "join, then watch it resolve." **Participation is paid, not just winning.** Disruption is **rate-limited** by cost (Crowd Control). Status comes from **seasonal or daily leaderboards with resets and energy caps.** Several of these games attach scarce, visible status rewards to top ranks.

### Cited Findings
**Marbles on Stream (Pixel by Pixel Studios)**
- The streamer picks a track and mode, then **opens registrations.** Viewers type `!play` while the on-screen prompt or countdown is showing. Typing the command when no race is open does nothing. (Guides and community posts.) — [Alucare install guide](https://www.alucare.fr/en/how-to-install-marble-on-stream/); [Steam community thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1170970/discussions/0/2277079183738772998)
- **Viewers do not control their marble after joining**; no other commands work. — [Steam community thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1170970/discussions/0/2277079183738772998)
- Developer patch notes describe viewers being entered into a visible list after joining, with the streamer able to control who may join. — [SteamDB patch notes](https://steamdb.info/patchnotes/8273929)
- **Points by mode** (official wiki): in Grand Prix lap races, the top 3 earn points; in Royale, only 1st earns points; in Tilted, every finisher of a level earns the same points and a failed level pays nobody. — [Racing wiki](https://wiki.pixelbypixel.studio/racing); [Royale wiki](https://wiki.pixelbypixel.studio/royale)
- **Season leaderboards** pay viewer prizes from 1st place (1,000 Marble Money) down to places 4-100 (30 Tournament Coins). Streamers also earn Champion Points, and 10 of them unlock a permanent emote slot. — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1170970/Marbles_on_Stream/)
- An **energy** system caps how much a viewer can earn: once energy runs out, wins stop earning points **until the daily reset.** This comes from a forum reply about 1,500 days old and may be outdated. — [Steam discussion "Question About Points"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1170970/discussions/0/3383914261435234167/)

**Stream Raiders (Sandbox Strategies)**
- The streamer is the "Captain," and viewers place units on the battlefield. One help page says players drop a unit **every 5 minutes until the battle is ready**, which is a rolling join window. — [Stream Raiders FAQ](https://www.streamraiders.com/faq-and-support/); [What the Stream review](https://www.whatthestream.com/2020/12/25/stream-raiders/)
- **Rewards are for participating:** viewers earn gold by taking part in battles and a scroll for a unit each time it is placed. Harder battles give a better chance at rarer scrolls, and each unit level adds HP and/or damage. — [Unit Leveling (fan wiki)](https://streamraiders.fandom.com/wiki/Unit_Leveling); [What the Stream](https://www.whatthestream.com/2020/12/25/stream-raiders/)
- **Developer statement (practitioner):** the game was designed as a **side-game played alongside the streamer's main content.** Players had no control of their units once the battle started; they "watched a simulation with their favorite streamer to see the outcome." — [Kevin Paskowski portfolio](https://paskowski.com/stream_raiders.html)

**Crowd Control (Warp World)**
- Viewers spend Bits on coins and buy effects. **Costlier effects limit how often disruptive ones fire**, which keeps the flow of incoming effects "manageable and fun for the streamer" (Twitch's official blog). — [Twitch blog](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2019/02/15/the-evolution-of-speedrunning-crowd-control-caf6e259e848/)
- The CEO said in an interview that the original UI/UX "was horrible" and was rebuilt so that new users need no instructions. — [Game Rant interview](https://gamerant.com/crowd-control-2-interview-new-features/)
- In developer docs, an effect that fails to execute **refunds the viewer's coins.** This mirrors Corrupted Raids' `refund: true` on refused redeems. — [Crowd Control dev docs](https://developer.crowdcontrol.live/unity/tutorial/first-effect.html)

**Jackbox audience mode (practitioner)**
- Quiplash (2015) expanded audience participation to **10,000 people**. The designer framed the audience as an "enhanced spectator" role in which audience members actually affect the outcome. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2015/06/30/quiplash-new-party-game-expands-audience-participation-to-10000/)
- The audience typically **votes as one aggregated group**, a "hive mind" in Poll Mine's Streamer Mode. In The Wheel of Enormous Proportions, a correct majority earns an audience slice on the wheel. Streamer features also include moderation and accessibility options. — [Jackbox blog, Party Pack 8](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/streaming-moderation-accessibility-features-jackbox-party-pack-eight); [Jackboxpedia (fan wiki)](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Audience)

**Choice Chamber**
- Choice Chamber is a platformer built to be played while streaming. Chat votes on what the streamer faces, and the IGF lists it. — [IGF](https://igf.com/choice-chamber); [Austin Chronicle](https://www.austinchronicle.com/screens/choice-chamber-11764706/)

### Inferences
- **Late joiners.** Every shipped example studied **closes** entry before the action resolves. Marbles registers people and then races; Stream Raiders places units until "ready." None lets joining mid-resolution change the outcome. For Corrupted Raids, joiners who arrive late in the gathering window raise `partyScaling`, making the enemy harder for everyone. Shipped patterns suggest three mitigations: (a) **lock the scaling headcount at a visible cutoff** (a countdown, then "gates closed"), (b) put latecomers in the *next* run, and (c) make sure a joiner's marginal contribution is positive, so that "more people" never feels like a penalty. The last is a design inference; no source measures it.
- **Pay participation, gate status on performance.** Marbles is instructive: Tilted pays every finisher equally (co-op participation reward), while Royale pays only the winner (competitive status). Corrupted Raids already pays casualties and defeats. A "Dungeoneer of the day" title is the status layer on top, the counterpart of the Royale-only payout.
- **Daily resets and caps** (Marbles energy) stop one heavy viewer from monopolising rewards and give everyone a fresh start each day. A daily title naturally resets, which the leaderboard research in Q3 favours.

### Gaps
- **No reliable source found on the Pokémon Community Game** (its catch-timer and participation design). The search returned nothing usable.
- No Marbles on Stream developer interview or postmortem was found. The Marbles findings come from the official wiki, the store page and community posts.
- No Streamer.bot or channel-point minigame design writeups were found.
- No shipped game was found that documents how it handles **difficulty scaling with late headcount**; this appears to be specific to Corrupted Raids.

## Q3. What does research say about social recognition and status as rewards (leaderboards, daily resets, "player of the day"), and what are the risks?

### Takeaway
Leaderboards motivate competitive, high-ranked people and **demotivate low-ranked and non-competitive people**, who disengage. Proposed fixes are **tiered or relative boards, upward-trend feedback, and resets**. Badges and titles steer behaviour, but only in a minority of users (about 5-30% on Stack Overflow). A single "player of the day" title is a narrow, top-heavy reward. It needs a broad participation layer beneath it, so that newcomers are not shown a contest they cannot win.

### Cited Findings
- **Competitiveness splits the effect.** On a learning platform, highly competitive users spent more time chasing top spots, while low-competitiveness users avoided participating (Amo et al. 2020, as summarised). Competitive learners treated the leaderboard as a motivator, and non-competitive learners reported demotivation or disengagement. — [ICCE: Examining the Effects of Leaderboards](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/download/841/781/1523); [arXiv 2512.08551](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08551)
- **Low rank is the main risk.** Public displays of poor performance are demotivating (Leung 2019) and can hurt self-esteem (Chan et al. 2018). Bottom-ranked students are less likely to keep trying to close the gap on an *absolute* leaderboard. — [arXiv 2512.08551](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08551)
- **Tier-based leaderboards** are proposed as a fix for demotivation at both the high and low ends (WSU dissertation, 2025). — [WSU Research Exchange](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/view/pdfCoverPage?instCode=01ALLIANCE_WSU&filePid=13426047850001842&download=true)
- **Position effects are not uniform.** In one gamified crowdsourcing study, lower positions sometimes raised motivation more than higher ones. Another study found the highest intrinsic motivation came from a high position **with an upward trend.** — [Emerald / Internet Research](https://www.emerald.com/intr/article/33/7/1/178330/How-leaderboard-positions-shape-our-motivation-the); [Univ. Augsburg "The winner takes it all"](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/127063/127063.pdf)
- Jia et al. (CHI 2017), "Designing Leaderboards for Gamification," studies how perceived leaderboard design affects motivation. The full findings were not retrieved. — [PDF](https://stephen.voida.com/uploads/Publications/Publications/jia-chi17.pdf)
- **Badges steer behaviour (Anderson, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg & Leskovec, WWW 2013).** A model of badge incentives matched aggregate Stack Overflow activity, with users increasing activity as they approached a badge threshold. — [WWW 2013 proceedings PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2013/proceedings/p95.pdf)
- **Badges steer only a minority (the "Phantom Steering" critique).** Only about **5-30% of users who earn badges appear to respond to them**, and the aggregate steering pattern can come from that small subgroup (Knowledge and Information Systems, 2021). A JASIST 2021 study also finds the effect is heterogeneous. — [Springer KAIS](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10115-021-01637-6); [arXiv / ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.06160); [JASIST 2021 PDF](https://tzin.bgu.ac.il/~omerlev/papers/jasist21.pdf)
- **In streaming, attention itself is the status currency.** Viewers compete for limited streamer attention as validation (Striner et al., as summarised). — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445511)
- **Marbles on Stream uses status rewards in practice**: seasonal top-100 tiers, and a permanent emote slot for streamers who accumulate wins. — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1170970/Marbles_on_Stream/)

### Inferences
- **"Dungeoneer of the day" (most dungeons survived today)** fits the research well in two ways. It **resets daily**, so no permanent hierarchy forms, and it is public, named recognition, which Hilvert-Bruce identifies as the core engagement driver. The research flags two risks. First, "most survived" rewards **volume of attendance**, so the same heavy viewer may win every day; the Marbles energy cap is one precedent for limiting this. Second, it is a single winner, which per the low-rank findings does nothing for everyone else and can discourage newcomers. Possible softeners, all inferences: show only the title-holder and never a full ranked list; use **survival rate** or a minimum-runs threshold; award "first clear of the day" or personal-best callouts so more people get named; and break ties by earliest achiever so the title is decided legibly.
- **A "most recently hit" roster ordering** is a low-cost attention mechanism. It puts names on screen in proportion to involvement in the fight, which matches the "compete for attention as validation" finding without creating a ranking anyone can lose. One caution: it also spotlights whoever is being *punished*. That is fine if being hit reads as "you're in the action," and not as "you're losing."
- The Phantom Steering result suggests not to expect a title to change most viewers' behaviour. It will motivate the competitive 5-30%. The broad base needs participation rewards, which Corrupted Raids already pays to everyone, casualties included.

### Gaps
- No study was found specifically on "player of the day" or daily-reset titles in live-streaming or games. The daily-reset reasoning is extrapolated from general leaderboard research and Marbles' energy reset.
- No research was found on newcomer retention under personal-best framing versus leaderboard framing; the search summary said so explicitly.
- Most of the leaderboard studies are small experiments in education or crowdsourcing. Whether they transfer to Twitch chat is untested.

## Q4. What principles produce simple, legible systems ("less is more," elegance, interesting decisions, theory of fun, spectator readability, cognitive load)?

### Takeaway
The practitioner canon agrees on several points. A choice is only worth showing if it is **interesting**: not always the same answer, and not random (Meier). Systems that are interesting once are not interesting ten times, so cut them (Meier on Civilization). **Constraints sharpen design** (Rosewater). **Fun is pattern-learning, so a mastered or unreadable system is boring** (Koster). Cognitive research puts working memory at about **four chunks** (Cowan), which bounds how many independent numbers a viewer can track at once. No peer-reviewed source was found on spectator legibility in particular.

### Cited Findings
- **Sid Meier, "Interesting Decisions," GDC 2012 (practitioner).** A game can be seen as a series of interesting decisions. A choice is *not* interesting if the player always picks the same option out of three, or if the choice is effectively random. — [Game Developer (Gamasutra) GDC 2012 writeup](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions); [The Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/gdc-2012-sid-meier-sees-interesting-decisions-even-in-rhythm-games/)
- **Meier on cutting:** Civilization's secondary technology tree was removed. "Something that's interesting you do once doesn't mean it's something interesting to do ten times." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions); [VentureBeat quotes](https://venturebeat.com/technology/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)
- **Mark Rosewater, "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons," GDC 2016 (practitioner).** The lesson list includes **"Restrictions breed creativity"** (usually listed as #18; one source calls it lesson 2, so the numbering conflicts) and "Leave room for your players to explore." — [Rapid Notes summary](https://rapidnotes.wordpress.com/2016/08/31/twenty-lessons-learned-over-twenty-years-of-designing-magic-the-gathering/); [Elite Game Developers summary](https://elitegamedevelopers.substack.com/p/magic-the-gathering-twenty-years-twenty-lessons-learned)
- A counter-view: a forum critique argues that the slogan skips the causal step, because restrictions by themselves only restrict (opinion). — [Riptide Lab forum](https://riptidelab.com/forum/threads/mordors-cube-the-ship-of-theseus.3180/page-5)
- **Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (2005, practitioner book).** "Fun is just another word for learning": fun is the satisfaction of mastering patterns. A game becomes boring when it offers no new patterns (once tic-tac-toe is solved, the challenge ends). It also fails if the patterns are too trivial or too hard to perceive at all. These points come from secondary summaries. — [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/19639-a-theory-of-fun-for-game-design); [Game Studies wiki](https://game-studies.fandom.com/wiki/A_Theory_of_Fun_for_Game_Design); [Dromedary and a half review](https://raphael-proust.github.io/games/a-theory-of-fun-for-game-design.html)
- **Cowan 2001 (research), "The magical number 4 in short-term memory."** Working-memory capacity is about **four chunks** in young adults, and less in children and older adults, revising Miller's "seven plus or minus two." Under information overload, chunks shrink to individual items. The fixed limit is disputed, and grouping (chunking) raises effective capacity. — [Cowan 2001, BBS (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf); [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/COWTMN); [Wikipedia: Magical Number Seven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magical_Number_Seven,_Plus_or_Minus_Two)
- A UX-principles site claims that interfaces showing **five or more discrete elements at once** exceed typical capacity. This is an **unsourced rule of thumb**; its companion statistic ("67% more errors") traces to no primary study. — [uxuiprinciples.com](https://uxuiprinciples.com/en/principles/working-memory)
- **Crowd Control (practitioner):** the UI was rebuilt so that new users need no instructions. The developer counts onboarding without instructions as a success criterion. — [Game Rant](https://gamerant.com/crowd-control-2-interview-new-features/)
- **Lessel 2022 (research):** options that interfere with the streamer were less favoured, and influence must suit the context. In other words, spectators value clarity of the main show over extra interactivity. — [preprint](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/Lessel-StreamersHell.pdf)

### Inferences
- **On the overlay, keep to about four things a viewer must track at once.** For example: level name and level, party health, enemy health, and one moment-to-moment element such as the latest hit. Group everything else so it reads as one chunk (the roster as a list, not as many numbers). This is extrapolated from Cowan; no HUD-specific study exists.
- **Levels too frequent.** Per Meier ("interesting once, not ten times") and Koster (repetition without new patterns is boring), a level-up that only grants two points and comes often becomes noise. Fewer, chunkier level-ups, each with a visible, distinctive payoff, fit the canon better. The AGENTS.md finding that XP runs about 1.8x budget points the same way.
- **Gear that feels rewarding.** Meier's test applies directly. If one gear option is always correct, the choice is not interesting. AGENTS.md's attribute-gating and negative `statMods` (a greatsword that costs Speed) create real trade-offs, the kind Meier means by an interesting decision. Koster's framing implies the *upgrade* is rewarding when it reveals a new pattern (a new role possibility, a visibly different sprite), and much less so when it adds +1 to a number already at 40.
- **Rosewater's restriction lesson supports keeping the settled constraints**: one shared fight, levels only, four stats. These were deliberate cuts, and the canon backs them.

- No source on "elegance" as a formal concept (for example, essays defining elegance as depth per rule) was retrieved.
