## 1. How commercial games scale group content by player count, when they lock it, and what happens on a late join

### Takeaway
Co-op games scale enemy toughness by headcount almost universally, usually sub-linearly. The difficulty TIER is always chosen by players before the activity: Hazard, mission difficulty, keystone, D3 difficulty or Monster Power. The HEADCOUNT multiplier is a different matter, and games split on when they sample it. WoW Flex samples headcount when the boss is engaged. Diablo II samples it when each monster is created. Monster Hunter: World re-scales live the moment a second hunter joins. Diablo II and Diablo III both explicitly pay out more for more players. Blizzard's stated Flex design goal reads almost as a description of the Corrupted Raids complaint: a group should never feel it has to turn a willing player away.

### Inferences
- **Three different "lock moments" exist in shipped games, and the tier is never one of the things that moves.** Flex samples headcount at encounter start. D2 multiplies per monster at creation, so a joiner changes only monsters not yet spawned. MHW re-scales the live monster on join. The thing players choose (Hazard, mission difficulty, keystone, D3 difficulty) is fixed before launch in every case. Corrupted Raids re-picks the *level* (its tier) on each join during gathering, and no precedent found does this.
- **Blizzard's Flex post is the most direct designer-side support for the proposal.** Its explicit goal is that nobody should "feel encouraged to turn away interested players", and it removed step-wise breakpoints for exactly that reason. A Corrupted Raids late joiner who bumps the level is a breakpoint in exactly this sense, and it lands on the people already committed. (This is designer opinion, not a measured result.)
- **Diablo II and Diablo III 1.0.8 pair added risk with added pay.** D2 raises XP by the same (n+1)/2 factor as HP. D3 1.0.8 cut per-player HP *and* added explicit multiplayer XP, Gold Find and Magic Find bonuses to make co-op more attractive. DRG instead ties reward to the chosen difficulty (Hazard Bonus), not to headcount. Both are coherent: Corrupted Raids could pay on turnout (D2/D3) or on the locked level (DRG).
- **Corrupted Raids' headcount HP curve is much flatter than any precedent.** At four players D2 gives 2.5x HP, D3 (1.0.8) 2.5x, MHW about 2.2-2.6x and DRG large-enemy resistance about 1.4x from solo. Corrupted Raids at 8 gives +15% HP (5% × 3 members past five) and leans on attack instead (+65% per doubling). This is not a flaw in itself: its fights hold dozens of auto-resolved bodies, not four players. But it means the precedents' numbers cannot be transplanted.
- **Small groups are where scaling complaints cluster.** The D3 cut, the Helldivers 2 small-group increase and its reversal, and Flex's floor at 10 all point the same way. In Corrupted Raids one joiner moves the mean most when the room is small.

### Gaps
- No primary per-player boss-health percentage for WoW Flex/Normal. Blizzard's quoted post gives the principle (health trails throughput), not a number. Wowhead was blocked.
- No Diablo IV per-player monster HP figure from any source.
- No developer source for DRG or Vermintide/Darktide mid-mission join behaviour, or for whether Helldivers 2's threshold updates instantly on hot-join.
- Capcom has published no MH multipliers. The MH Wilds leave behaviour is contested between guides.
- D2's drop-rate steps (3/5/7) and the pre-1.10 XP formula could not be re-verified (diablowiki and purediablo blocked).

## 2. What academic DDA research finds, and whether visible or announced adjustment harms fairness or enjoyment

### Takeaway
The foundational DDA papers favour *off-stage* changes to content not yet encountered, and they warn that on-stage changes look erratic. The best evidence on *telling* players is that it does not hurt and may help (Denisova & Cairns: 42, 120 and 60 participants). The one controlled multiplayer study of locked versus live adjustment (Zeng & Sweetser 2022, n=23) found players explicitly did not want enemies to change strength mid-fight. One reason was that "their work was lost". Performance-based DDA reliably improves *performance* but not clearly *enjoyment* (Robb & Zhang 2022, n=221). It can also inflate confidence (Constant & Levieux 2019, n=138).

### Cited Findings
- [read] **Hunicke & Chapman (2004), "AI for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Games"**, Challenges in Game AI AAAI Workshop, San Jose (citation per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_game_difficulty_balancing)). Hamlet runs on Valve's Half-Life engine and uses inventory theory. Goals: "We want Hamlet to intervene just enough – so that system behavior is relatively stable and predictable over time" and to "Execute changes as seamlessly as possible." "Reactive actions will adjust elements that are 'in play' or 'on stage'… Proactive actions will adjust 'off stage' elements (i.e. entities that are spawned but inactive or waiting to spawn)… health, accuracy, damage… of entities that have yet to appear on screen." The trade-off: "Reactive changes run the risk of disrupting the player's sense of disbelief… causing the game to appear schizophrenic. Proactive changes, because they happen 'in the wings', are less likely to interrupt the player's suspension of disbelief." Proactive changes "are harder to evaluate" and can trigger "a spiraling loop of change". There is no user evaluation in this paper, only a planned heart-rate study. — [Hunicke & Chapman PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf)
- [abstract read via OpenAlex] **Hunicke (2005), "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games"**, ACE 2005: "Conventional wisdom suggests that while players enjoy unpredictability or novelty during gameplay experiences, they will feel 'cheated' if games are adjusted during or across play sessions." It "offers preliminary evaluation results which challenge common assumptions about player enjoyment and adjustment dynamics." The full results were not read. — [OpenAlex record, DOI 10.1145/1178477.1178573](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/1178477.1178573)
- [read] **Jennings-Teats, Smith & Wardrip-Fruin, "Polymorph: Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment Through Level Generation"**, PCGames workshop 2010 (Monterey). Levels are "procedurally generated as the player moves through the level, one chunk at a time as needed… This is not to say that the player will never die in a tough section… but the game will correct for this in the next section." The difficulty model comes from players rating roughly 10-second segments from "1-Easy through 6-Hard". The authors "completed a pilot study with more than one hundred undergraduate game design students" and planned "thousands of participants". — [PCG workshop PDF](https://www.pcgworkshop.com/archive/jennings-teats2010polymorph.pdf). A companion paper, "Polymorph: A Model for Dynamic Level Generation", appeared in AIIDE 2010 (6(1):138-143) and reports "the results of the user study on which Polymorph's model of level difficulty is based". — [AAAI OJS](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12417)
- [read] **Denisova & Cairns (2015), CHI PLAY note.** Between-subjects, 42 participants. A hidden adaptive timer (which could stretch play to 108s) versus a standard timer. The adaptive group was more immersed: total IEQ 121.05 vs 113.50, F(1,40)=7.41, p=0.010. "No participants reported noticing the change in the speed of the timer, nor did the participants in the two conditions differ in their level of perceived challenge." — [Denisova & Cairns 2015 PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~pc530/pubs/Denisova_CHIPLAY15.pdf)
- [read] **Denisova & Cairns (2019), "Player Experience and Deceptive Expectations of Difficulty Adaptation in Digital Games"**, *Entertainment Computing*, pp. 56-68 (doi 10.1016/j.entcom.2018.12.001). Study 1 had 120 participants, Study 2 had 60 and ran for about 20 minutes. The adaptive timer raised immersion (F(1,114)=22.37, p<0.001, ηp²=0.164). The abstract: "players felt more immersed in the game when told that the game adapts to them, regardless of whether the adaptation was present… it remained prominent even during longer gaming sessions… at least in some circumstances, developers do not need to be concerned about negative effects of telling players about in-game adaptations." — [White Rose eprint 139704 (PDF)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/139704/1/1_s2.0_S1875952118300521_main.pdf)
- [NOT VERIFIED; secondary press] In a *Don't Starve* study, 21 volunteers told a session was "adaptive" rated it harder. — [Inverse](https://inverse.com/article/6944-study-finds-gamers-can-t-tell-adaptive-ai-from-the-placebo-effect)
- [read] **Constant & Levieux (2019), CHI** (doi 10.1145/3290605.3300693). An in-game bet of 1-7 measured confidence in three games (logical, motor, sensory). 138 participants (80 in the DDA "Balanced" condition, 58 in "Random"), median age 15, at the Cité des sciences in Paris. The two conditions ran about a month apart with new participants (not randomised concurrently). Result: "players seem to be much more overconfident with a difficulty adapted to their skills." The authors also hypothesise: "when facing unpredictable difficulty levels, players may be more careful with their estimation of their success chances. They know they can be surprised by the difficulty of the next turn." — [HAL hal-02141897 (PDF)](https://hal.science/hal-02141897/document)
- [read] **Zeng & Sweetser (2022), "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in a Multiplayer Minecraft Server"**, OzCHI '22. This is the only controlled lock-versus-live comparison in a multiplayer game found in either pass. Each mob's level is a Gaussian distance-weighted *average* of nearby players' strength. A mob "recently damaged" is not re-levelled. Scenario 2 adjusted mobs "once immediately after spawning… then remain unchanged". Scenario 3 also re-adjusted "at certain intervals". 23 participants played in pairs. Enjoyment was 4.2/5 (Scenarios 1-2) vs 4.3/5 (Scenario 3). In open responses for Scenario 3, the most common answers included "hoping mob strength didn't change (13)", and "the participants did not want the mobs to suddenly become stronger or weaker while fighting them. If a player was in a situation where they were about to kill a mob and it suddenly leveled up, the player could feel that their work was lost." Limitations, per the authors: small sample, two players per session, scenarios not counterbalanced. — [ANU repository PDF](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/4ae20c15-6ca4-4171-ac7a-ae5393491e0b/download)
- [read, abstract page] **Robb & Zhang (2022), *Acta Ludologica* 5(1):4-23.** 221 participants played a DDA version, a constant version or an incremental version. "Results show that the DDA group performed significantly better. However, there were no significant differences in terms of enjoyment or experience of flow." — [Acta Ludologica](https://actaludologica.com/?p=1527)
- [abstract read via OpenAlex; full text blocked] **Zohaib (2018), "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) in Computer Games: A Review"**, *Advances in Human-Computer Interaction* 5681652. On static difficulty: "The features such as frequency, starting levels, or rates can be set only at the beginning of the game by choosing a level of difficulty. This can, however, result in a negative experience for players as they try to map a predecided learning curve." — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1155/2018/5681652)
- [NOT VERIFIED; Anubis-blocked] *Co-Op Craft* (TU Darmstadt master's thesis, 2016) compared three DDA algorithms for heterogeneously skilled groups in a StarCraft II-engine co-op game, with DDA on and off. — [TUprints 5492](https://tuprints.ulb.tu-darmstadt.de/5492/)
- [NOT VERIFIED; carried over] Missura & Gärtner (DS 2009; NeurIPS 2011) treat DDA as player-type prediction and online learning. — [NeurIPS 2011](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/4302-predicting-dynamic-difficulty)
- [NOT VERIFIED; carried over, designer opinion] Black Rock's *Pure* team on rubber-banding: "its unfairness is easy to spot", and players "want to feel like they are playing with the same rules as their opponents". — [MCV](https://www.mcvuk.com/development-news/black-rock-studio-blasts-arubber-bandinga/)

### Inferences
- **Hamlet's on-stage/off-stage split maps directly onto Corrupted Raids.** The gathering-window banner is "on stage": everyone watching sees the level and has decided to join on it. Changing it in response to one person is a *reactive, visible* change of the kind Hamlet says can make a game "appear schizophrenic". Locking the level and applying any remaining headcount effect only to the not-yet-started fight is the "in the wings" option.
- **Zeng & Sweetser is the closest evidence to the actual complaint, and it points to locking.** Its players did not want enemies' strength to change mid-encounter and felt their "work was lost" when it did. The system's own design also refused to re-level a mob under attack. Corrupted Raids' fight is auto-resolved, so the analogue of mid-encounter is the gathering window after a viewer has committed. The evidence is weak (n=23, qualitative, no counterbalancing) but it points the same way as Hamlet and Flex.
- **Announcing is safe, and silently shifting is not the same thing.** Denisova & Cairns show that *telling* players about adaptation does not reduce, and may raise, immersion. Their adaptations, though, were invisible in-play (nobody noticed the timer). Corrupted Raids' jump is visible, attributable to one named viewer, and adverse to everyone else. Those studies do not cover that case.
- **Live adaptation buys performance, not necessarily fun.** Robb & Zhang (n=221) found that DDA improved scores but not enjoyment or flow. The cost of a jumpy banner is therefore paid in legibility for a benefit that may not reach enjoyment at all.
- **Constant & Levieux's "unpredictable difficulty makes players cautious" hypothesis** suggests a level that can change at the last second may make viewers hedge, whether by joining late or not at all. This is the authors' untested hypothesis plus my inference.

### Gaps
- No study found on visible versus hidden difficulty change in *spectator* or *Twitch-chat* games, or with more than two players per group.
- Hunicke (2005) results were not read (ACM blocked, no author copy found).
- Co-Op Craft could not be read.

## 3. Flow theory, target win rates, challenge versus frustration, and loss aversion

### Takeaway
Flow-in-games theory (Chen) puts the player's *sense of control* on equal footing with challenge-skill balance. Large-scale data (Brändle et al. 2025, about 2.2M votes over about 261k levels) confirms a preference for intermediate difficulty. It adds two effects that bear directly on the complaint: enjoyment peaks where difficulty *matches what the player expected*, and players are motivated by success on easy levels. No game-specific optimal win rate exists in the research. "85%" comes from a learning-theory paper. Loss aversion does carry over into games (300-participant study), but no study links it to difficulty jumps specifically.

### Cited Findings
- [read] **Jenova Chen, "Flow in Games", MFA thesis (USC).** It lists three core elements: the game is intrinsically rewarding; it "offers right amount of challenges to match with the player's ability"; and "The player needs to feel a sense of personal control over the game activity." On DDA: "Every so often, DDA systems take control away from the game designers, which potentially causes more problems than a linear game." He contrasts "Passive Flow Adjustment" (system-oriented DDA) with player-oriented "active DDA" through choices embedded in gameplay. — [Chen thesis PDF](https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/Flow_in_games_final.pdf). The CACM 2007 article itself was not read this pass.
- [read] **Brändle, Wu & Schulz (2025), "Leveling up fun", *Scientific Reports* (doi 10.1038/s41598-025-14628-2).** Datasets: a puzzle game (7,994 levels, 376,341 votes), a racing game (138,662 levels, 614,770 votes) and Super Mario Maker (115,032 levels, 795,313 votes, from 32,665,615 attempts). "people preferred levels of intermediate difficulty in all games… players preferred levels that matched closely with their prior expectations of difficulty and were also motivated by success." The difficulty-expectation disparity shows an inverted U whose "peak… lies at a difference of 0 — where the expected difficulty matches the calculated difficulty measure". Players also showed "'success' from completing levels with low difficulty". These were confirmed in two controlled experiments. No win-rate target is given. — [Europe PMC full text, PMC12488971](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12488971/fullTextXML)
- [read] **Roohi et al. (2020), "Predicting Game Difficulty and Churn Without Players"**, CHI PLAY '20 (Angry Birds Dream Blast data). It cites learning-theory work "identifying 85% success rate as optimal", namely Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019), "The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning", *Nature Communications* 10. It reports "our data is more in line with Lomas et al. [CHI 2013], who found lower difficulty to yield the highest engagement in their educational game", while noting that a Lomas follow-up found moderate difficulty most motivating when players could choose their opponent. — [arXiv 2008.12937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.12937)
- [read, thesis record] **Klarkowski (2021), "Presence and Effects of Loss Aversion in an Adventure Video Game"**, University of Saskatchewan thesis (with Gutwin). A Zelda-style game; "three experiments with a total of 300 participants, consisting of wagering gold at different win:loss ratios… players still exhibited a strong bias towards avoiding losses in all experiments… designers should understand and account for loss aversion when setting up risk and reward structures in their games." — [USask record](https://harvest.usask.ca/signposting/describedby/2d0daa40-da4f-4783-8892-6f1272bff45f); handle [10388/13478](https://hdl.handle.net/10388/13478)
- [read, abstract] Game theory on reference points (Driesen, Perea & Peters): in loss aversion equilibria the "reference points—points below which they consider payoffs to be losses—are endogenous". This is theory, not a player study. — [Glasgow eprint 77917](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/77917)
- [NOT VERIFIED; popular press] The "70-85% success Goldilocks zone". — [Abandon the Script](https://abandonthescript.beehiiv.com/p/are-we-failing-enough)

### Inferences
- **The expectation-match finding is the strongest research basis for locking.** Brändle et al. find that enjoyment peaks where actual difficulty equals expected difficulty. A viewer who joined on "LEVEL 2" and fights LEVEL 3 has had the expectation broken *after* committing. Locking the level, and showing the locked level before most joins happen, keeps expectation and reality equal.
- **Chen's "sense of personal control" argues that a viewer's join should change only their own stake.** Joining is a choice. Making it also raise everyone else's risk takes control from the rest of the room.
- **Loss aversion plausibly makes an upward bump worse than an equal step down is good.** Klarkowski shows that loss aversion survives into virtual stakes (300 participants). The Driesen line places the reference point where expectations sit, and the banner level is that expectation. Applying this to difficulty steps is still an *inference*: no study tested difficulty jumps.
- **Win-rate targets remain designer choices.** The 85% figure is about optimal *learning* in a model, not enjoyment in games. Lomas and Roohi suggest that engagement often peaks at *easier* settings, and Brändle finds that success on easy levels motivates. For a stream, where a wipe is public, that argues against targets near 50%, especially for the starter dungeon.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, game-specific optimal win rate.
- No study of loss aversion in response to a difficulty increase (as opposed to wagers).
- Csikszentmihalyi's primary texts and Chen's CACM article were not read.

## 4. Group-composition rating (average vs total vs max), and keeping one strong player from condemning or carrying a weak group

### Takeaway
In PvP rating research the MAX member best predicts a team's result (Dehpanah et al., IEEE CoG 2021, over 100,000 matches). Apex uses HIGHEST to rate premades, explicitly as anti-smurf protection, and admits it makes it "very difficult for new players to join in with their more skillful friends". In PvE fairness, Corrupted Raids' MEAN is the safe direction. The closest academic PvE analogue (Zeng & Sweetser's Minecraft DDA) also uses a weighted average of nearby players.

### Cited Findings
- [read, abstract] **Dehpanah, Ghori, Gemmell & Mobasher, "Evaluating Team Skill Aggregation in Online Competitive Games"**, IEEE Conference on Games 2021. The paper proposes two new aggregation methods and compares them with "a standard approach extensively used in the research literature", using Elo, Glicko and TrueSkill on "three real-world datasets including over 100,000 battle royale and head-to-head matches". "Our evaluations show the superiority of the MAX method over the other two methods in the majority of the tested cases, implying that the overall performance of a team is best determined by the performance of its most skilled member." — [arXiv 2106.11397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.11397)
- [read] **Respawn (Apex Legends, 2023).** Closest-average balancing is how solo players are formed into teams in Pubs ("We can form teams with the closest average skill to balance the match. We take this approach in Pubs."). For a *premade*, Respawn lists LOWEST, HIGHEST, AVERAGE and WEIGHTED AVERAGE, and "Apex Legends matching algorithm currently uses HIGHEST for Pubs and Ranked. It offers the best protection against matchmaking exploits with new players and smurfs… This, however, makes it very difficult for new players to join in with their more skillful friends." Planned: premades get "small advantages that should be added to their team's skill rating". (This replaces the earlier note's "conflict": the two statements cover different cases.) — [EA: Apex matchmaking 2023](https://www.ea.com/en-gb/games/apex-legends/news/matchmaking-2023)
- [read] **Zeng & Sweetser (2022)**: each mob's level is a "Weighted average level… computed based on the contribution weight", with weights from a Gaussian of player distance. — [ANU PDF](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/4ae20c15-6ca4-4171-ac7a-ae5393491e0b/download)
- [read] **WoW Flex** keys scaling on headcount, assuming joiners' "skill/gear level matches the rest of the group's". It does not rate gear. — [Warcraft Wiki: Flexible Raid](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Flexible_Raid)
- [NOT VERIFIED] Diablo IV World Tier 4 world bosses scale to the average level of the party. — [Game8](https://game8.co/games/Diablo-4/archives/415872)

### Inferences
- MAX predicts PvP results *because strong players carry*. Corrupted Raids' AGENTS.md shows the same carry in its own data (one level-150 among fifty level-1s lifts the room one level, and everyone still wins). So the mean *under*-rates a carried room, which is the safe direction for a PvE fight. Apex's own admission that HIGHEST makes it "very difficult for new players to join… with their more skillful friends" is exactly the failure Corrupted Raids' design rejected.
- The late-joiner shift is arithmetic: with n members at mean m, a joiner rated r moves the mean by (r − m)/(n+1). In a room of 5, one veteran rated 10× the mean moves it by 1.5m. In a room of 50 the same veteran moves it by about 0.18m. Ways to damp it while keeping the mean: snapshot it at a lock point; cap any one member's contribution at k × the room median; use a trimmed mean; or add hysteresis, so the level moves only if the rating clears a threshold by a margin. These are design inferences, not sourced practice.

### Gaps
- Which "standard" and second new method Dehpanah et al. tested, and the effect sizes, were not read (abstract only).
- No source found on how PvE group finders (WoW LFR item level, FFXIV, Destiny) aggregate *gear/power* for enemy scaling.

## 5. Concrete patterns for late-join fairness: difficulty locks, smooth versus step scaling, and reward that scales with turnout

### Takeaway
The verified evidence supports three patterns. (1) **Lock the tier before commitment.** Every game reviewed has players choose the tier up front. Flex samples headcount at boss engage. D2 applies headcount only to monsters created later. Hamlet prefers off-stage changes. The one controlled multiplayer test found players did not want strength to change mid-fight. (2) **Let headcount move only smooth, sub-linear numbers, and remove breakpoints.** Blizzard made Flex abilities scale by weighted randomisation ("No more breakpoints!") so that no one is turned away. D3 cut per-player HP from +70% to +50%. (3) **Pay for turnout.** D2 scales XP with headcount exactly as it scales HP. D3 1.0.8 added explicit multiplayer XP, Gold Find and Magic Find bonuses. DRG instead pays on the chosen difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- [read] Flex samples headcount "at the time the bosses are encountered" and later replaced step breakpoints with weighted randomisation, because "good players aren't being turned away from groups because of the notion that their presence will make things harder for everyone." — [Warcraft Wiki: Flexible Raid](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Flexible_Raid)
- [read] D2 multiplies HP "when a monster is created", and XP by the same (n+1)/2. — [Arreat Summit](https://classic.battle.net/diablo2exp/monsters/basics.shtml)
- [read] D3 1.0.8: per-player HP +70% → +50%, plus "Explicit bonuses… giving players XP, Gold Find, and Magic Find boosts" in multiplayer. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2013-04-03-diablo-iii-gets-massive-multiplayer-improvements-in-1-0-8.html)
- [read] DRG: players select Hazard before the mission, it sets the Hazard Bonus reward, and player count scales enemy count, damage and some resistances. — [DRG Wiki](https://deeprockgalactic.wiki.gg/wiki/Hazard)
- [read] MHW re-scales HP "the moment there is more than 1 player in a hunt", with an on-screen message when a late joiner arrives. Iceborne made it scale back down at one player. Damage to players is unaffected. — [Fextralife MHW](https://monsterhunterworld.wiki.fextralife.com/Multiplayer)
- [read] Helldivers 2's attempt to raise small-group patrol pressure was reverted in the next major patch. — [1.000.300](https://helldivers.wiki.gg/wiki/1.000.300); [1.000.400](https://helldivers.wiki.gg/wiki/1.000.400)
- [read] Hamlet: proactive (off-stage) changes "are less likely to interrupt the player's suspension of disbelief." — [Hamlet PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf)
- [read] Zeng & Sweetser: Scenario 2 (fixed at spawn) versus Scenario 3 (re-adjusted at intervals); players hoped "mob strength didn't change". — [ANU PDF](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstreams/4ae20c15-6ca4-4171-ac7a-ae5393491e0b/download)
- [read] Brändle et al.: enjoyment peaks where actual difficulty equals expected difficulty. — [PMC12488971](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12488971/fullTextXML)
- [read] Denisova & Cairns: telling players a game adapts did not hurt immersion and raised it. — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/139704/1/1_s2.0_S1875952118300521_main.pdf)

### Inferences (options for Corrupted Raids, simplest first)
1. **Lock the level at a fixed point in the gathering window** (for example halfway, or T−15s). The banner reads "POORS — LEVEL 3 · LOCKED", and later joiners enter that level. This matches the Flex pattern (sampled at a commit moment) and Hamlet's off-stage rule, and it keeps Brändle's expectation equal to reality for everyone who has already joined.
2. **After the lock, allow at most a downward move, or none.** This removes "someone joined and it got harder" entirely. If it ever moves, it should only be able to make things easier for the committed.
3. **Make turnout pay, legibly.** Following D2 and D3 1.0.8, apply a headcount reward multiplier to XP and gold, in a few clear steps so it reads on the banner (for example "12 joined: +30% loot"). Keep the existing smooth `partyScaling`, or reduce it. That curve already follows the Flex principle that enemy scaling trails group throughput. Joining then visibly helps the room, which is the outcome Blizzard's Flex post aims for. Because of loss aversion (Klarkowski), frame it as a bonus gained rather than a penalty avoided.
4. **If live re-reading stays before the lock, damp it.** Options: hysteresis margins around `BAND_THRESHOLDS`, a per-member cap at k × room median, or a trimmed mean. The precedent is Blizzard's breakpoint removal, which turned a step into an expected value. Corrupted Raids' within-level ramps (`squadFor` body count, stat-multiplier slide) already do this inside a level. The complaint is the level boundary, and a lock addresses that most directly.
5. **Announce rather than hide.** "The room's power set tonight's fight at LEVEL 3" is consistent with Denisova & Cairns and with the project's "measured, legible" premise. Since the change is visible and attributable, hiding it is not an option anyway.
- This all fits the "less is more" premise: one lock moment, one headcount reward, and no hidden re-rating. That is fewer moving parts on screen than the current re-read on every join.

### Gaps
- No A/B test or survey data from a shipped co-op game comparing locked and live difficulty. Zeng & Sweetser (n=23, two players) is the only controlled comparison found.
- No developer documentation of mid-mission join behaviour for DRG, Helldivers 2 or Vermintide/Darktide.
- No source on turnout-scaled reward in Twitch or audience-participation games (Marbles on Stream, Stream Raiders and similar). Not searched in either pass.
